How do I know it's solid and not plated?

Three ways, and you can do all of them before you pay. Look for the stamp — 10K, 14K or 18K, struck into the clasp or the inside of the band. Put it on the scale — plated brass weighs about half what solid gold does for the same size, and we'll show you the reading. Ask for the acid test — it takes a minute, we do it at the counter, and it costs nothing.

If a shop won't do all three in front of you, that tells you what you need to know.

Why is yours cheaper than the mall?

Because a mall counter pays mall rent, and because most of their price is markup on a piece they bought finished from a wholesaler. We cast and set in the same room we sell in, and we price off the metal weight plus labour rather than off what the tag can bear.

It also means we can't discount. There's no inflated number to come down from — the price you see is already the floor.

What does VVS actually mean — and do I need it?

VVS is a clarity grade: very very slightly included. It means that under 10× magnification a trained grader can just barely find an internal flaw. It is two grades above the point where anything is visible to your eye.

So: no, you almost certainly don't need it. The honest answer is that VS stones look identical on a chain at conversational distance and cost meaningfully less. We'll sell you VVS if you want it — we'd just rather you knew what you were paying the premium for.

Lab-grown or mined — which am I getting?

Lab-grown, unless the tag says otherwise. They are chemically and optically the same material, they test as diamond because they are diamond, and they cost a fraction of the mined equivalent.

Where a piece uses mined stones we say so on the tag, we name the supplier on the receipt, and the price reflects it. We don't mix the two in one piece without telling you.

What happens when a stone falls out?

Bring it in and we reset it, free, for as long as we're open. Same for re-tipping worn prongs, re-soldering a link, resizing a band, and re-polishing a piece that's gone dull.

This isn't a warranty with conditions attached — there's no registration, no receipt required, no window. We set it, so we fix it. The only thing we charge for is replacing a stone that was lost rather than broken, and that's at cost.

Will it turn my neck green?

Not from solid gold. Green skin comes from copper in low-grade alloys and from the base metal under worn-through plating. 10K has more alloy than 14K, so if you have reactive skin, go 14K and up.

If a piece you already own is doing it, bring it in — we can usually tell you what it's made of in about two minutes, whether or not you bought it here.

Can I trade in the chain I've got?

Yes, if it's solid. We weigh it, test the karat, and credit you the metal value at the day's fix minus a refining margin we'll show you on paper before you agree to anything.

We don't take plated or filled pieces, because there's nothing to recover. We'll still test them for you and tell you what you've actually got.

How long does a custom piece take?

Four to six weeks for most things, longer if the setting is complicated. Anyone promising you a fully custom, hand-set piece in 48 hours is either selling you something pre-made or having it done somewhere they can't tell you about.

You'll see a render before we cast, and you don't pay the balance until you've approved it.

Three things we won't tell you.

A page of answers is only worth reading if it's also honest about where the answers stop. These are the questions we get asked that we won't give you a clean number for.

  • What it'll be worth later

    Nobody knows where gold trades next year, and any jeweller quoting you an appreciation figure is guessing at best. Buy it because you want to wear it.

  • That it's an investment

    Retail jewellery sells above melt and buys back below it. That spread is real and it doesn't close quickly. Treat it as something you own, not something you hold.

  • What another shop's piece is worth

    We'll test the metal and tell you the karat and the weight. We won't put a resale number on someone else's work — we don't know what was paid or what's inside it.
